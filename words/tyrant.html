The tyrant of the virtues

My field, math, is largely right at the point of panicking about the effects of AI, both in terms of the careers of mathematicians and the joy of doing math. I am lucky and unlucky to be studying math right now—I’m seeing real uncertainty in my future earlier than most, but I’m also asking the questions that everyone in science needs to be asking rather soon. I’ve been thinking about this almost constantly for a few weeks. Here’s something interesting I’m having trouble grappling with:

An imbalance of power

If you were to approach me today and tell me “you’re physically weak,” I don’t think I would find any hard time coming to terms with it. I’ve always been a smaller guy, and I go to the gym to enjoy myself, not to become bigger or stronger. Besides the unexpectedness of that interaction, I wouldn’t pay much attention to its memory. I think this is true of most people in our time. Physical strength is a good quality that people can have, and that they can work towards—a virtue— but it’s not one that our world really cares about. Your strength will not get you a job, or grant you higher status, or earn you more respect from others. The same is true of most of our other virtues:

  • Your discipline can help you work towards your goals and achieve them, but it largely doesn't show itself socially. No one will measure you according to discipline, and no one will compare themselves to your discipline.
  • Your compassion may be recognized by people close to you, but will hardly affect your place in the world.
  • Your creativity could lead you to fare well in some careers and subcultures, but those are rare. Most exceptionally creative people aren’t noticed.
  • Your honesty will, more often than not, end up a hindrance to your professional life.
  • Your sociability is no longer a point of pride. It won’t affect your performance at work or school.
  • Your courage is rarely necessary; necessity itself is a foreign concept to us now.

These qualities are all profoundly good elements to one’s personal life. The world we inhabit, however, must evaluate us; we must base our sense of who is “desirable” on some combination of the virtues. In my lifetime, this combination has been shaped, above all, by intelligence. The other virtues are of comparatively little importance.

Hence, if you were to approach me today and tell me “you’re stupid,” it would come off a little differently. Even in writing, stupid seems to ring; it's a dirty utterance. I'd wager that very few young people would take such an insult well. Today, questioning someone’s intelligence is received worse than questioning their trustworthiness, or their friendliness. Those other virtues are hardly even seen as something to strive towards.

Sometimes, people will openly flaunt their lack of some other virtue, so long as their intelligence is not questioned. For instance, as a lack of sociability tends to express, "I'm an introvert. I'm not really a social person. I don't like people." Similarly, a lack of discipline expresses, "Look at my screen time, it's sooo bad." We betray the other virtues in service of just one. How did our intelligence reach such a position? How did it become the tyrant of the virtues?

The tyrant’s past

In the ancestral environment, we needed intelligence—but we much more direly needed the other virtues. Your survival may depend on your ability to run after an animal, or win the respect of peers, or fight to defend others, or tell a good campfire story. Forgetting the virtues behind these abilities led only to death. As we developed civilizations, upper classes developed which were isolated from the daily struggles for food and safety. Those people probably aspired to intelligence, among other traits, but we know from their writing that they still had more important virtues in mind. A human was something which could act on the world, develop goals, choose good or evil, or connect with the divine.

The industrial revolution changed this. In a world of machines, we no longer valued the physical “push” of man. Our role was now to think; we identified the human as the one who thinks. Thinking solved problems for us—thinking could invent new processes, or organize our efforts, or help us provide more bounty for everyone. Before this, many important people were associated with intelligence—the great conquerers were students of strategy, the great politicians were students of philosophy, and the aristocracy were uniquely educated. But only in the industrial age can the common person could become more successful by being smarter. Today we have the ability to move up—score a better job, start a business, or seek education—but this ability is contingent on intelligence. If we want to succeed in this way, we all must serve the tyrant.

A loyal subject

For most of my own life, my identity relied heavily on intelligence. This is true of many or most university students—we pursued science because it was respected, and we got upset as children when we didn't score the highest on an exam.

I remember feeling particularly lost when I entered middle school. I had considered my intelligence as the salient thing about me—when I looked in a mirror I saw the smartest kid at the school. When I inevitably discovered someone who was far ahead of me in learning math and physics, I felt my role was stripped away from me. I denied; I told my friends that she wasn’t “really smart” and that she “just worked hard at it”. I worked; I tried to find ways to get further and further “ahead”. I panicked; I wondered what my friends thought of me without my role. Over time I ended up rationalizing my way to restoring this identity, and a sense of superiority. I wasn't able to accept a vision of myself without intelligence—I served the tyrant.

I spent more of my free time learning; I wanted to feel I was becoming smarter, more distinguished. I felt at home in a classroom, for it was where I would feel recognized; I studied math and physics because I saw them as "smarter" than the other fields. I looked up to people with careers that proclaimed their intelligence—researcher, mathematician, quant, CEO, physicist. I wanted my intelligence to make me special; I wanted it to prove my worth and my humanity.

The tyrant’s future

Unfortunately, the 2020s seem like they might change the way we see our humanity. Of course, there’s presently speculation and controversy surrounding our AI advances, but it seems reasonable that our ability to reason mathematically or scientifically is not a unique human quality. Earlier I noted how harsh it is to call someone dumb. We all strive to show our worth and our humanity—and in our world, doubting one's intelligence is doubting their worth.

It's clear that overemphasizing any virtue or ability that is not specific to humans is a bad path: Someone overly strong or bulky becomes a beast; someone overly obedient or persistent becomes a machine. We insult these types of people because their singular virtue is shared by something else in the world—it's not uniquely human.

Presently we are emerging towards a world in which intelligence is no longer uniquely human. What we regarded as the most prized of our abilities, the logical problem-solving process, may now be brought down to worldly entities. I predict this will change our perception of problem-solving in our lives. The scientists are no longer the pinnacles of that human endeavor of reasoning—they are imitations of the reasoning machines.

It is therefore very plausible that our societies will dictate a rearrangement of our virtues. If problem-solving jobs are done by new machines, then we will no longer, on a large scale, seek to train our problem-solving process; we will no longer collectively aspire to the virtue of intelligence. Perhaps our jobs and positions of prestige will be determined less and less by intelligence and more and more by some other combination of virtues—honesty, agency, friendliness, creativity, discipline, or something else. Our collective aspiration will steer towards some of these, and people will feel superior or inferior to others based on them, more so than on intelligence. Perhaps words like liar or hermit will be our new worst degradations, replacing stupid.

So long as it is replaced by a more balanced combination, we will end up celebrating and aspiring to more of the virtues with which we are blessed. This I perceive to be a good thing. We should identify with more aspects of humanity. When we look back upon our history, we should not forget our connection to the valor of the warriors and soldiers, the diligence of the monks and scholars, the passion of the protesters and revolutionaries, the originality of the artists and philosophers, the integrity of the judges and rulers, or the kindness of the mothers and fathers and lovers and friends. We should not let our tyrant, intelligence, blind us to these connections. I hope to see a future in which the tyrant is overthrown.
