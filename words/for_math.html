An argument for doing math

"You want a PhD in math? Now?"

I don't just hear this question from my friends; it has become a permanent echo in my mind. It is the noise of the city outside that remains when I embrace the quiet. It is the note that resonates perfectly between the bare walls when I sing in the shower. It's a question that deserves an answer.

A mathematician grows

When I was young, mathematics was not a path that demanded an explanation. People learned math because they were good at it, it was respected, and it could make a good living. I liked the way it felt to solve a problem, and the way it felt when a new intuition grabbed hold of me. That was that, and so I started down the long path of doing math.

In my first experience in the research world, working under a particle physicist, I didn't have to give anyone a speech on why I did it. It was cool, and it was what smart people did. That experience was my first introduction to studying anything in an advanced fashion, and it changed how I saw myself. I spent all my summer days and summer nights with a textbook, Introduction to Elementary Particles; to me, that book was a portal to a new world. It showed me a place where I could reach out to all sorts of new ideas and see how far my understanding could grow. I was greedy to learn, and in physics I found a vast territory to explore. As I worked, however, I found the borders of this territory, and quickly became annoyed. In particular I remember studying symmetries in quantum mechanics, seeing the significance of Lie groups like SU(2) and SO(3), and being told that their secrets lay in the field of abstract algebra. At this I was dismayed. Perhaps I should’ve been happy where I was, in physics—but my mind wasn’t happy accepting that the deeper secrets of algebra don’t belong to me. Instead I told myself I must head in that direction. I started seriously studying math simply because I couldn't stand to ignore it.

When I joined a lab working on quantum computing, or started taking advanced courses in combinatorics, I only needed to explain to my family what the words quantum computing and combinatorics meant, not why I felt drawn to them. When I found a well-paid internship in quant finance, no one ever asked "are you sure you want to go into that field?". Even now, as I prepare to submit my first paper, it's tough to express where my drive to work comes from.

But something changed in my outlook during these years. As I explored various fields, I started looking towards the questions my mind asked naturally. I let my questions bring me to new concepts and experiences, and in time I narrowed my interest. Working on a programming language compiler taught me that I actually didn't like engineering and I needed to see the math ideas underneath it. Courses in algorithms taught me a new way of looking at "problems" and "solutions", and it was intuitive and fun! A course in graph theory brought me to the wonderful world of combinatorial structures, and a forward-thinking mentor brought me to think deeply about AI in math.

A voyage into uncertainty

I'm driven towards math—I like learning, teaching, working on problems, and finding new ways to think. I want to explore and specialize in combinatorics, because I delight most in it. I want to find new problems to solve. I love math, in the sense that I love all the ways I've experienced math so far. I want to be a mathematician, someone who develops math for their own enjoyment and for the service of our society.

But that question still rings in my ears, unrelenting; it still feels unanswered.

I can't head into a PhD program because I like the way math has been. I'm going to change in the next several years, and in today's world it's quite clear that the practice of math will change drastically. I don't know where these changes will lead us, only that it won't be what I'm used to. Math is headed for rough, unpredicable seas, and my question is whether to venture into those seas. If I want to work in math now, I need a reason beyond my love for the subject—a call to a voyage into uncertainty.

I believe I have one: The uncertainty itself is a part of what I'm looking for. I'm dedicated enough to research math even when it seems strange; I'm ambitious enough to push my field in a new direction; I'm diligent enough to work on a problem for a long time, and I'm spirited enough to seek out a long tradition of researchers to join. I want to set off into the sea, and I want to become a mathematician.
