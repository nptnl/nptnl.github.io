A statement of purpose

October 25, 2026 - I started writing a grad school application and spun out into this. Probably too personal to submit there.

"You want a PhD in math? Now?"

I don't just hear this question from my friends; it has become a permanent echo in my mind. It is the noise of the city outside that remains when I embrace the quiet. It is the note that resonates perfectly between the bare walls when I sing in the shower. It's a question that deserves an answer.

A mathematician grows

When I was young, mathematics was not a path that demanded an explanation. People learned math because they were good at it, it was respected, and it could make a good living. I liked the way it felt to solve a problem, and the way it felt when a new intuition grabbed hold of me. That was that, and so I started down the long path of doing math.

When I tried my first hand in research, working with a particle physicist, I didn't have to give anyone a speech on why I did it. It was cool, and it was what smart people did. That experience was my first introduction to studying anything in an advanced fashion, and it changed how I saw myself. My summer nights were spent with a textbook, Introduction to Elementary Particles; to me, that book was a portal to a new world. It showed me a place where I could reach out my arms and touch all sorts of new ideas and see how far my own understanding could grow. I wanted to grasp things at the most general level I could: after reading about symmetries in quantum mechanics, and being told their secrets lay deep in a scary place called abstract algebra, I told myself I must head in that direction. That's when I became a math major.

When I joined a lab working on quantum computing, or started taking advanced courses in combinatorics, I only needed to explain to my family what the words quantum computing and combinatorics meant, not why I felt drawn to them. When I found a well-paying internship in quant finance, no one ever asked "are you sure you want to go into that field?". Even now, as I prepare to submit my first combinatorics manuscript, it's tough to express whether my work comes from a drive to accomplish, or a deliberate choice on how to spend my time.

But something changed in my outlook during these years. As I explored various fields, I started looking towards the questions my mind asked naturally. I let my questions bring me to new concepts and experiences, and in time I narrowed my interest. Working on a programming language compiler taught me that I wanted to go see how mathematical ideas underlied data structures. Courses in algorithms taught me that discrete math was, for me, the home of the most intuitive and fun problems and techniques. A course in graph theory brought me to the wonderful world of combinatorial structures, and a forward-thinking mentor brought me to think deeply about AI in math.

A voyage into uncertainty

I've developed true interests; I find myself driven to attack combinatorial problems. I want to gain a better understanding of how forbidden subgraphs or minors affect a graph, or understand the thresholds of properties of random graphs, or see how combinatorial structures help develop the other sides of math. I love math, in the sense that I love all the ways I've experienced math so far. I want to be a mathematician, someone who develops math for their own enjoyment and for the service of our society.

But that question still rings in my ears, unrelenting; it still feels unanswered.

I can't head into a PhD program because I like the way math has been. It would be foolish to hope that nothing will change. I'm going to change in the next several years, and in today's world it seems quite clear that the practice of math will change drastically. I can't predict where these changes will lead us—but I can predict that they will take us somewhere.

Math is headed for rough seas, for an outcome of high variance. My question is whether to venture into those seas; I cannot pretend the field of math will stay constant. If I want to work in math now, I need a reason beyond my love for the subject—a call to a voyage into uncertainty.

That reason came to me as a sudden clarity, a beautiful silence drowning out my question: The uncertainty itself is a part of what I'm looking for. I'm dedicated enough to research math even when it seems strange; I'm ambitious enough to push my field in a new direction; I'm diligent enough to work on a problem for a long time, and I'm spirited enough to seek out a long tradition of researchers to join. I want to set off into the sea, and I want to become a mathematician.
